Why "130% of the float is shorted" doesn't mean a squeeze

  1. The float number is out of date. Data sites still list about 1.07 million shares in the float and 2.47 million outstanding. But 220 million shares traded in a single day this week. The company has been issuing new shares through its at-the-market program, so the real float is far larger and the real short percentage is far smaller. Finviz itself shows 37% short, and even that is figured on the old share count.
  2. Shorts can get out in minutes. The short ratio is 0.04 days: at normal volume, every short seller could buy back in roughly 20 minutes of trading. A squeeze needs short sellers who can't find shares to buy, and here there are plenty.
  3. The company sells into every bounce. The +19% and +24% days on Nov 11–12 came on 220 million shares each, then the stock fell 27% the next day. That is what an active at-the-market offering looks like: rallies become a chance for the company to sell more stock.

The filings

  • 424B5, Nov 13 and Oct 13: prospectus supplements that let the company sell new shares.
  • 8-K, Nov 3: the filing behind the $100 million equity sales program.
  • DEF 14C, Oct 7 (preliminary Sep 25): an information statement, meaning insiders approved an action by written consent instead of a shareholder vote. Insiders own about 94%. With the stock far below Nasdaq's $1 minimum, a reverse split is the likely next step.
  • Insiders: no buying in the last 90 days.

The only positive news in the last 30 days was a Nov 3 press release about delivering electric trucks under government incentive programs. The stock fell 85% that same day.

Bottom line

On small companies with an active share-sale program, high short interest usually means short sellers expect more dilution, and so far they have been right. If a reverse split comes, these stocks often keep falling after it.
